## 2026-09-21 (Lecture 9, human cognition and evaluation)

The evaluation lecture. The lecturer says the report needs a justification for the two methods chosen, and that "there's no right answer": the argument is what is marked.

**What the evaluation must contain**
- Name the **specific problems** users found, say what you did about each, and show **concretely where it changed in the interface** with annotated screenshots. You do not have to act on everything users say.
- Users are good at spotting problems and poor at proposing solutions, so decisions about fixes are the designers' job.
- A small sample can give conflicting feedback; subjective data is only a weakness "when you don't have sufficient volume to find saturation and patterns". Different users hit different problems, which is why you need several.

**Observation method (5 participants). Pick one:**
- **Think aloud:** give a task and ask them to talk as they go. Easy to run and shows where people get stuck, but speaking changes performance and the data is subjective. You take notes and do not interrupt.
- **Cooperative evaluation:** think aloud plus you may ask questions and they may ask you.
- **Post-task walkthrough:** they do the task, then you replay and ask why. Good for finding reasons, but people may invent explanations; better where talking during the task is not possible.
- Recording audio **with consent** is recommended, not video. AI transcription is fine ("it shouldn't be used to interpret, it should be used to transcribe"). Screen recording is another option.

**Querying method (a different 5 participants). Pick one:**
- **Interview:** informal and flexible, allows follow-ups and finds unexpected problems, but is subjective and slow to analyse. Prefer open questions, avoid long or jargon-heavy questions, and keep the demographics of participants balanced.
- **Questionnaire:** quick, analysable, but shallow and not flexible. He recommends a **standardised instrument** rather than inventing one: the **SUS** (10 items rated 1 to 5, scored 0 to 100, with reversed items) for usability, or **NASA-TLX** for workload. Decide what construct you are measuring first.
- The query method should **complement the observation method**: collect what observation could not, not the same information twice.

**Choosing methods (the framework he gave):** stage of the process (ours is design stage); controlled versus in the wild (he says **use controlled** for A3); subjective versus objective; qualitative versus quantitative; how detailed; how obtrusive; and available time, equipment and expertise.

**Other methods named but not required:** heuristic evaluation (a checklist), cognitive walkthrough, review-based evaluation (compare against similar apps in the market, which he says is a good optional quick check), physiological methods (eye tracking and similar), contextual logging, diary studies.

**Q&A on a synchronous, multi-user system (directly relevant to ChronoMap)**
- A Figma prototype cannot connect several real users. Use **exemplar scenarios**: pretend another user is acting (for example, "this was just dropped here by another user") and make clear in the interface and in the participant instructions that the system is synchronous.

**Schedule given in this lecture:** that week's tutorial covers Figma; the break week has nothing; weeks 10 and 11 are open feedback lectures ("bring a sketch, ideas for videos, ideas for evaluation"); the later tutorials cover how to make a good video and how to do good evaluation.

# Tips from the tutorial and feedback slides

Source: `docs/course-materials/` PDFs (Tutorials 9, 10, 11 and the Lecture 10 and 11 feedback decks). The slides are mostly text plus example images. Lectures 10 and 11 are only prompts for group discussion and contain no extra requirements.

## Tutorial 10, Evaluating Social Systems (most useful for the report)

- **Expected prototype progression:** `Mid-fi (A2) → High-fi 1 → Evaluation → High-fi 2`, or in the slide's words wireframes → mockup 1 → eval → mockup 2. Structure the Results section along this line.
- **Usability heuristics: "In your A3 report, use and document how you apply these heuristics."** This is an explicit instruction, so it should appear in the report. The ten are Nielsen's: visibility of system status; match between system and real world; user control and freedom; consistency and standards; error prevention; recognition rather than recall; flexibility and efficiency of use; aesthetic and minimalist design; help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errors; help and documentation. Source given: nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics. (Our existing design log touches several, for example error prevention in the quorum and moderation features and recognition over recall in the briefing screen.)
- **Usability definition to quote:** how well a specific user, in a specific context, can use a product to achieve a defined goal effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily.
- **Think-aloud, how to run it:** find a representative set of users; give several representative tasks; the user does them **alone**; tell them mistakes show the system's limits, not theirs; **do not offer solutions** when something goes wrong, let them resolve it.
- **Cooperative evaluation:** analyst and user work through it together; make the participant feel like a product owner; discuss problems **and ask them to propose solutions**.
- **Post-task walkthrough:** record use (screen, video or notes), then ask the user to reflect on specific events afterwards; **introduce the context** of the event you are asking about.
- **Interviews:** prepare questions but keep it a human conversation, with follow-ups such as "Can you explain this further?".
- **Questionnaires:** usually after a set of tasks; validated ones such as the **SUS** allow quantitative analysis.

## Tutorial 9, how to make a demo video (due date has passed, but the advice shows what was marked)

- Worth 5 of 55 points. A story or narrative is "very important". Focus on the **problem first, then a quick intro of the solution**, and give more time to the unique and important features. Do not try to explain everything.
- Draft a storyboard; each panel could be acted out. Plan location, actors and equipment.
- Record short clips with several takes. For screen recordings make cursor movement visible and not too fast.
- Voiceover: quiet room, a script, **your own voice, not an AI voice**, and add captions.
- Edit: rough cut first, sync voice, then trim; keep sound clips below the voiceover; pace it.

## Tutorial 11 and Lectures 10 and 11

- Just working sessions and a final chance for feedback on design and mock-ups; the slides add no new requirements. Questions go on Ed.

# Close reading of the handout against the rubric and the draft
